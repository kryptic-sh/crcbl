what is actually running

A T-handle in zero gravity, flipping.

The orange handle is spun about its middle axis — the one whose moment of inertia sits between the other two — with a tiny nudge about another. That spin is unstable, and the handle turns itself over and back again for as long as the page is open. It is the Dzhanibekov effect, and nothing on this page drives it: it falls out of the inertia tensor the two boxes add up to and the gyroscopic term in the rotation equations.

The counters are the claim. A free body keeps its angular momentum and its energy, and a simulation that did not would show it here as a handle slowly settling into a steady spin. The integrator solves the gyroscopic step by the implicit midpoint rule, which conserves both, and the panel shows how far each has drifted, in trillionths.

A box dropped flat, landing flat.

The blue box is dropped with no spin and lands on its four lower corners, level to a trillionth, sunk a fraction of a millimetre into the floor: every contact is a stiff spring, and the box's weight compresses it. The box is put back at the top every four seconds to land again. A box against another box is the next rung, so nothing here asks for one.

The obstacle wall.

Balls, and every seventh drop a pill, fall onto a board of pegs, tilted bars and turned wedges between a back board and an invisible front pane, and bounce down into the bins. The counters are what the contact solver did: pairs the broadphase is tracking, contacts begun and ended, the time each stage took, the deepest overlap, and the mean bounce against the restitution the materials asked for. The wall holds a hundred and twenty bodies and takes the oldest away as each new one drops. No cubes fall: a cube landing in a bin is a box against a box.

A thousand balls in a pit.

Waves of five balls pour into a walled pit until there are a thousand, and the pile comes to a stop. Nothing sleeps yet, so a still pile is solved in full every tick — that is the rung after next — and the pit neither overflows nor clears, which is a later rung's scene. The step times on the panel read "no clock" here, because the page's physics reads none; the browser's figure is the whole frame's, timed around the call from outside and logged to the console as a [TIMING] line.

The hash is the same everywhere. Nothing in a physics step calls a platform's maths library: the operations it uses are ones every processor performs identically, and the sine and cosine it needs are built inside the engine from them. So the state hash all three rooms reach ten seconds in — whichever room is on screen — is the very number the native build's test pins, and the browser test checks the two agree.
